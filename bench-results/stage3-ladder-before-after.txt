mdh matching engine: a tick ladder with a hierarchical occupancy bitmap (Stage 3)

The change
----------
The price index was a std::pmr::map<Price, LevelSlot> per side: finding a level meant a
red-black tree descent, and finding the touch meant walking to one end of it. It is now a flat
array indexed by tick, over a band of prices, with a three-level occupancy bitmap over the top:

  LevelSlot ladder_[band];        // uninitialised; meaningless unless the bitmap says otherwise
  uint64 occupancy_[band/64];     // one bit per tick
  uint64 mid_summary_[band/4096]; // one bit per occupancy_ word
  uint64 summary_;                // one bit per mid_summary_ word

Finding a level is now an array index. Finding the touch is three dependent loads and three
count-leading-zeros, over 1 KB of bitmap that stays in L1. Stepping to the next level is a
masked word plus, usually, no summary lookup at all.

Prices outside the band go to a std::pmr::map, which is exactly stage 2's structure, and the
walks merge the two indexes. bench-results/stage3-ladder-band-decision.txt is the measurement
that chose that policy over rejecting out-of-band prices or sliding the band, and chose the
8192-tick band and the 8 MB whole-engine budget that scales it down across a large universe.

Because the map-only path is stage 2 unchanged, before/after here is one binary with the
budget set to zero and the same binary with it set to 8 MB -- same allocator, same session, one
variable. The "before" arm therefore also pays the in-band branch, which slightly understates
the change; everything else about the comparison is tighter than a rebuild would be.

Correctness: all 407 tests pass (9 new). The two that matter are differential: the existing
400,000-operation MatchingBookStress comparison against an independent std::map reference model
now also runs at a price spread five times the band, so four prices in five are out of band and
every best-price and every walk is a live merge of the two indexes, and again with no ladder at
all, which is what a large universe gets.

A level no longer allocates. In-band levels are array slots, so creating and destroying price
levels is now allocation-free -- visible below as two book shapes that used to differ in
footprint reporting identical figures.

Run-to-run variance: medians of three runs, on an otherwise-loaded laptop. Most rows reproduce
within a few percent; the cancel-scaling rows do not, and are marked.

This is the row the memory budget was designed for: 64 instruments each get the full 8192-tick

useful band and books fall back to the map -- which is why the 20,000-instrument pipeline test
still costs what it always did.

The FOK preflight rows are the clearest single result in this stage. crossable_quantity walks
levels from the touch until the price bound stops it, and walking N levels used to be N tree
hops through pooled nodes scattered across memory. It is now N steps through a bitmap that fits
in L1, and at 1024 levels that is 45 microseconds down to 5.

Three separate things, worth keeping apart.

The depth-1 replace regression is real and structural. That benchmark leaves exactly one order
on the book and repeatedly cancels and re-adds it, so every iteration empties a side and
re-creates it. Creating and destroying a level costs six dependent accesses across three arrays
(the tick, its occupancy word, its summary word) where a one-node map costs a single pooled
node already in cache. The ladder's fixed per-level cost is higher; it wins by making the
*count* of levels stop mattering. The crossover is visible in the same family: 1 level is 24%
worse, 16 levels is 38% better, 256 levels is 66% better.

The FOK rows at zero and one level are the same effect at 11 ns: about 1.7 ns of extra
indirection on an operation that has almost nothing else in it.

The priority-preserving replace rows move by about 1 ns at every depth. That path does not
touch the price index at all, so this is not work -- it is code layout, and it is at the edge
of what these runs resolve. Recorded rather than explained away.

      orders    shape                before    after    change
        1000    1 order/level         221.9    215.6     -2.8%
        1000    100 orders/level      150.5    215.6    +43.3%
       10000    1 order/level         253.8    235.9     -7.1%
       10000    100 orders/level      181.1    186.8     +3.1%
      100000    1 order/level         277.7    275.6     -0.8%
      100000    100 orders/level      209.0    208.9     -0.0%
     1000000    1 order/level         208.0    207.8     -0.1%
     1000000    100 orders/level      140.5    140.3     -0.1%

A ladder is a fixed cost per book -- 64 KB of array plus 1 KB of bitmap per side that has ever
held an order -- and it replaces a per-level cost of a 48-byte pooled map node. So the sign of
the change depends entirely on how many levels the book has.

The two 1000-order rows landing on the same figure is the point: a thousand orders spread over
a thousand levels and a thousand orders stacked ten deep now cost the same, because in-band
levels are array slots and no longer allocate. The 43% on the shallow row is the ladder's fixed
65 KB divided by only a thousand orders; by ten thousand it is 3%, and by a hundred thousand it
has disappeared. A book too small to amortise a ladder is also a book too small for any of this
to matter.

Allocations per operation are unchanged at 0.000-0.003, and the histogram for a million-order
book is unchanged at ~560 allocations: this stage moves bytes between structures, it does not
change how often the allocator is reached.


What this stage did not do
==============================================================================================
Out-of-band books are unchanged, by construction. The 65,536-, 262,144- and 1,000,000-level
rows spill 94%, 98% and 99.6% of their levels into the map and measure within a few percent of
where they started. That was the prediction in the band decision and it held.

The band does not slide. A side re-anchors when it empties, which costs nothing and handles a
book that drains and re-forms elsewhere, but a book that drifts steadily out of its band will
degrade to map performance rather than following the market. The workload generator anchors
prices to a static mid, so there is no drift here to tune against -- inventing one would be
measuring my own guess. The overflow map is what makes that a graceful degradation instead of
an outage, and it is what would make sliding an optimisation rather than a rewrite.

Stage 3's targets in the plan were extrapolated from pre-slab numbers and are not all reachable
from here: cancel at 4096 levels was projected 184 -> 40-60 ns, but stage 2 had already taken it
to 85, and this stage takes it to 64. The two stages together are what the projection was
about.
