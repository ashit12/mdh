mdh matching engine: choosing the price band for the tick ladder (Stage 3, decision)

The question
------------
A tick ladder is an array indexed by price, so it needs a bounded band of prices to cover.
The plan left three options open, to be decided from measured level distributions rather
than guessed at: reject out-of-band prices, keep an overflow map for them, or slide the band
with a reference price. This is the measurement and the decision.


What the books actually look like
---------------------------------
From bench_matching_workload's new "Price-level distribution" section. Span is the distance
in ticks between the lowest and highest occupied price on a side; levels is how many of those
ticks hold an order; "in band" is the share of resting orders within that many ticks of the
touch.

scenario                                   orders    levels       span   in 1024   in 8192
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
realistic workload, 1 instrument             8918        74         84    100.0%    100.0%
realistic workload, 16 instruments           1342        67         85    100.0%    100.0%
realistic workload, 64 instruments           1033        60         86    100.0%    100.0%
benchmark seed, 1024 levels                  1024      1024       1024    100.0%    100.0%
benchmark seed, 4096 levels                  4096      4096       4096     25.0%    100.0%
benchmark seed, 65536 levels                65536     65536      65536      1.6%     12.5%
benchmark seed, 262144 levels              262144    262144     262144      0.4%      3.1%
benchmark seed, 1000000 levels            1000000   1000000    1000000      0.1%      0.8%

Two things stand out.

The realistic workload is tiny and dense: 84 ticks of span holding 74 occupied levels, so
88% of the ticks in its range are in use. That is close to the ideal case for a ladder --
almost none of the array would be wasted -- and a 1024-tick band covers it twelve times over.
The caveat is that this generator anchors passive prices to a static mid, so it has no drift
by construction; a real instrument's band moves during a session and this measurement cannot
say anything about how fast.

The benchmark's own scaling rows are the opposite, by design: they seed one order per tick
across up to a million consecutive ticks to push the price index until it breaks. They are
not markets, but they are what the rest of the suite measures, so which of them a ladder
covers decides which rows it can move.


The decision
------------
1. Out-of-band prices go to an overflow std::map, consulted only when it is non-empty.

   Rejecting them is a behaviour change, and the measurement says it would be a large one:
   it would reject the majority of the orders in four of this suite's own scaling rows, and
   several tests place orders far from any anchor. Real exchanges do band prices, but they
   do it as a risk control with published limits, not as an artefact of a data structure --
   and adopting it here to avoid writing a fallback would be letting the implementation
   choose the semantics.

   A sliding band is the option the measurement cannot justify. Its purpose is to follow
   drift, and this workload has none, so anything I built would be tuned against a constant.
   It also would not help the wide rows: no anchor covers a million-tick span. If drift is
   ever measured, sliding is the natural follow-up, and the overflow map is what makes it
   optional rather than urgent.

2. The band is 8192 ticks per side, scaled down by a whole-engine memory budget.

   8192 is where the curve above stops paying: it covers the realistic workload with a
   factor of ninety-five to spare and the 1024- and 4096-level rows completely, while the
   next row up (65,536) would need a 512 KB-per-side ladder and still leave two rows out.

   A ladder costs instruments x band x 2 sides, which is exactly why stage 1 came first.
   The engine divides an 8 MB budget across its universe and rounds down to a power of two:

     instruments      band      per instrument     total
               1      8192            128 KB      128 KB
              64      8192            128 KB        8 MB
             128      4096             64 KB        8 MB
             512      1024             16 KB        8 MB
           1024+      none                 -            -

   Below 1024 ticks the band stops being worth the fixed cost, so the book falls back to
   the map alone -- which is stage 2's structure, still correct and still measured. This is
   what keeps the 20,000-instrument pipeline test affordable: those books get no ladder.

3. Ladders are allocated per side on first use and left uninitialised.

   A level's contents are only meaningful where the occupancy bitmap says so, so the array
   never needs zeroing and its untouched pages are never faulted in. Only the bitmap (1 KB
   per side at full band) is zeroed. An instrument that never trades costs nothing, and one
   that trades on one side pays for one side.

4. An empty side re-anchors on its next insert.

   Not a sliding band -- no re-anchoring of live orders, no cost on any hot path -- just the
   observation that a side with nothing in it has no reason to keep its old base. A book that
   drains and re-forms somewhere else recovers its ladder for free.


What this predicts
------------------
The realistic workload and the 1024/4096-level rows should move; the 65,536-, 262,144- and
1,000,000-level rows should not, because they will be in the overflow map, and the honest
expectation for them is "unchanged, plus a predictable branch".

Worth restating a caveat from the stage 2 report: the plan's stage 3 targets were extrapolated
from the *pre-slab* tree cost, and the slab already took cancel-at-4096-levels from 155 ns to
80 by shrinking the map node. The remaining headroom is smaller than the plan assumed.
